How to Write a European-Standard CV That Actually Gets Accepted

8 min readUpdated 2026

A European recruiter spends just a few seconds on each CV before deciding whether to keep reading or move on. Your CV isn't a place to list everything you've ever done — it's a tool to convince them quickly that you deserve an interview.

CV Writing

What Is Europass?

Europass is the standardized framework the EU promotes for formatting CVs, designed to make it easier to compare candidates from different backgrounds and countries. It isn't mandatory everywhere, but following its structure gives you a CV that looks familiar to any European recruiter, reducing the odds of being screened out for formatting reasons alone.

The Standard CV Structure

  • Personal details: name, contact info, and city/country only — no need for a full address or marital status.
  • Short professional summary: two or three lines summarizing who you are, your field, and your top achievement.
  • Work experience: reverse chronological order (most recent first), with concrete results, not just duties.
  • Education: degree, institution, and year.
  • Languages: using the standardized European levels (A1 to C2).
  • Technical skills and additional certifications.

Do You Need a Photo?

It depends on the country: some European countries (Germany, traditionally) commonly included a photo, while international companies and the tech sector in general now tend not to ask for one — some even prefer you leave it out to avoid bias. Safe rule: don't add a photo unless the job posting or local custom clearly calls for it.

Describing Your Experience So It Convinces

Instead of writing "Responsible for sales," write what you actually achieved: "Increased monthly sales by 20% over 6 months by reorganizing a 5-person team." Use strong verbs (developed, led, achieved, reduced) and numbers wherever possible — that's what separates a CV that gets read from one that gets skipped.

Languages and Their Levels

Don't just write "good" or "fluent." Use the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR): from A1 (beginner) to C2 (near-native). If you're unsure of your level, free online assessment tests can give you a reliable number to put on your CV.

The Cover Letter

Many European jobs, especially in France and Germany, expect a cover letter alongside the CV. Keep it short (half a page maximum), and tailor it to each company: why this company specifically, and what you bring to them — not the other way around.

Common mistakes to avoid: one CV sent to every job without adjustment, spelling errors in the target language, unnecessary personal details (religion, marital status), and a CV running past two pages without good reason.
