Legitimate Jobs in Europe for Foreigners: How to Verify an Offer Before You Apply

7 min readUpdated 2026
General Guide

Important: This guide is for information and safer job-search planning. It is not legal, immigration, or financial advice. Work authorization and recruitment rules differ by country. Confirm every important detail with the employer, the destination-country authority, and the official visa channel.

What makes a European job offer legitimate?

A legitimate job offer is more than a professional-looking PDF or a recruiter’s message. It should connect a real employer, a real role, a real work location, a clear contract, and a lawful hiring route. If one part cannot be verified, the offer is incomplete no matter how attractive the salary sounds.

Foreign applicants need a higher verification standard because relocation involves travel, documents, fees, and legal status. A vacancy can be genuine but unsuitable if the employer does not hire from abroad, the role does not support a permit, or the contract does not match the destination-country route.

EURES provides job-search support, adviser access, labour-market information, and services related to living and working abroad across 31 countries.1 Use official employment services as a discovery channel, then verify the exact employer and legal route.

The five-part legitimacy test

Before sending sensitive documents or paying any fee, verify these five parts.

Part What you should be able to verify Warning sign
Employer Registered name, website, address, and independent contact Only a messaging-app profile or generic email
Vacancy Exact title, duties, location, hours, and supervisor “Europe job” with no workplace or duties
Contract Salary, deductions, duration, probation, and benefits Contract arrives only after payment
Recruitment route Named agency, fee policy, and employer relationship Pressure to pay for a guaranteed offer
Work authorization Country-specific route and responsible authority “Visa guaranteed” with no permit category

A legitimate offer can still be a poor job. The test measures verifiability, not job quality or immigration approval.

How to verify the employer

Start with the employer’s official website. Check whether the company lists the same role, location, and contact details. Use a phone number or email from the official domain, not only the details supplied by a recruiter. Search for the legal company name and compare it with the name in the contract.

Look for consistency. The company’s industry, address, job duties, salary, and hiring person should make sense together. A small mismatch does not prove fraud, but it is a reason to ask for clarification before proceeding.

Do not treat a company logo, registration number, or LinkedIn page as conclusive proof. Verify the employer through a government company register or recognized employment service when available in the destination country.

How to verify a recruitment agency

Recruitment agencies can be legitimate, but the applicant should know whether the agency is acting for the employer, a subcontractor, or the applicant. Ask for the agency’s legal name, registration, address, website, fee policy, and the identity of the employer.

A trustworthy agency should explain what it does, how it is paid, what costs are official, and what happens if the employer withdraws the vacancy. Be especially cautious when the agency refuses to let you contact the employer or asks you to pay for an invitation letter, guaranteed visa, or reserved job slot.

Use a written question set:

  1. Who is the legal employer named in the contract?
  2. Which company pays the salary?
  3. Where will the work physically take place?
  4. Which authority handles the work authorization?
  5. What costs are paid to an official authority, and which are agency fees?
  6. What happens if the permit or visa is refused?

A recruiter who cannot answer basic questions should not be trusted with your passport or money.

The vacancy-verification score

Score the offer from zero to ten. Give two points for each category that is independently verified: employer, vacancy, contract, agency, and work authorization.

Score Meaning Recommended action
0–3 Major information is missing Stop and do not pay or send sensitive documents.
4–6 Possible lead but material gaps remain Request written evidence and verify independently.
7–8 Stronger evidence base Confirm the final contract and official route.
9–10 Highly documented offer Still check the destination authority before travel.

The score is a risk-control tool. It cannot guarantee that a company will keep a promise or that an authority will approve a permit.

What a contract should explain

Read the contract before accepting the offer. It should identify the employer, work location, job title, start date, duration, salary, pay frequency, working hours, overtime, leave, probation, deductions, accommodation, transport, and termination conditions. Ask for a translation you understand if the contract is not in a language you can read confidently.

Compare the contract with the vacancy and the visa application. If the advertised role is “IT specialist” but the contract describes casual labour, ask why. If the salary changes after you pay a fee, stop the process. If accommodation costs are deducted, request the exact amount and conditions in writing.

For non-EU applicants, the contract may be one part of a work-authorization process rather than the final permission to work. The non-EU jobs guide explains why an offer, a permit, and a visa should be treated as separate checkpoints.

How to verify work authorization

Start with the destination-country government or the European Commission’s EU Immigration Portal. The portal organizes work and longer-stay information by country and category because immigration rules are shared between the EU and Member States.2

Ask the employer which permit or residence route applies to the exact job. Confirm who submits the application, which documents you provide, whether the occupation is regulated, and what you are allowed to do while the application is pending. Do not rely on “the company handles everything” without a route name and official reference.

The France work permit guide and Italy work permit guide show why country-specific sequences matter. France and Italy do not use identical employer, visa, quota, or arrival procedures.

Common job and visa scams

A scam often combines urgency, secrecy, and payment. The recruiter may say that only a few slots remain, that you must pay today, or that the embassy requires a private transfer. The offer may promise a salary far above local market rates and a visa without an interview or contract.

Other red flags include a free email address for a supposedly major employer, a request to use a personal bank account, an invitation letter sold as a job, a passport request before verification, and a refusal to name the workplace. One red flag may have an innocent explanation; several together should stop the application.

Do not pay for a guaranteed job, visa, residence permit, or embassy appointment. Do not falsify documents or let an intermediary complete forms you do not understand. Save messages, contracts, payment requests, and website addresses if you need to report the matter.

A safer application workflow

Apply through the employer’s official career page or a recognized employment service whenever possible. Keep a record of the vacancy URL, date, contact person, contract version, and permit information. Ask questions in writing, and do not allow pressure to replace evidence.

Frequently asked questions

How can I find legitimate jobs in Europe for foreigners?

Use official employment services, recognized job portals, employer career pages, and verified agencies. Then confirm the employer, vacancy, contract, recruitment route, and work-authorization path independently.

Do legitimate employers ask applicants to pay for a visa?

Applicants may pay official visa or document fees in some circumstances, but a private demand for payment for a guaranteed job or guaranteed approval is a serious warning sign. Confirm any fee with the official authority.

Can a recruitment agency guarantee a European work visa?

No agency can replace the decision of the responsible immigration authority. A legitimate agency can explain the process and support documentation, but it should not promise approval.

Should I send my passport to a recruiter?

Do not send identity documents to an unverified contact. Ask why the document is needed, who will hold it, how it will be protected, and whether the official process requires it at that stage.

Final takeaway

Legitimate jobs in Europe for foreigners are built on verifiable facts. Confirm who the employer is, what the role is, what the contract says, how the recruiter is paid, and which official route authorizes the work.

A careful verification process may slow the application, but that is safer than paying for a job that does not exist or travelling with a document that does not authorize employment.
