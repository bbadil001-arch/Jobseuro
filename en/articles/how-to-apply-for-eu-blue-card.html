How to Apply for the EU Blue Card: A Country-by-Country Checklist

7 min readUpdated 2026
Visas

Important: This is an informational guide, not immigration or legal advice. The EU Blue Card is issued by national authorities under EU and national rules. Requirements, salary thresholds, fees, documents, and procedures vary by Member State and can change. Verify the destination country’s official rules before applying.

What is the EU Blue Card?

The EU Blue Card is an employment and residence route for certain highly qualified workers from outside the EU. The European Commission explains that the applicant generally needs higher professional qualifications or, where national rules permit, qualifying higher professional skills, together with an employment contract or binding job offer for at least six months and a salary meeting the applicable country threshold.1

The Blue Card is not a Europe-wide job-search permit. It is connected to a specific Member State, employer, job, and set of conditions. Denmark and Ireland do not participate in the EU Blue Card route described by the European Commission, so applicants targeting those countries must check their national alternatives.1

The EU Blue Card application checklist

Prepare the application in this order.

Step Evidence or decision Why it matters
1. Choose the country Select the Member State where the job will be performed The national authority and detailed rules are country-specific.
2. Confirm the job Contract or binding offer for highly qualified employment A general interest in working in Europe is not enough.
3. Check duration Contract or offer must meet the applicable minimum duration The Blue Card is linked to qualifying employment.
4. Check qualifications Degree, certificate, or permitted professional experience The evidence must match the occupation and national rules.
5. Check salary Compare the offer with the current national threshold The threshold is set in relation to national rules.
6. Check documents Passport, visa if required, insurance, translations, and forms Missing or inconsistent documents can delay or prevent the application.
7. Submit correctly Apply to the competent national authority or through the employer where allowed The submission channel differs by country.

The checklist is a preparation framework. The destination authority makes the actual decision.

Who may qualify?

The European Commission describes the Blue Card as a route for highly qualified employment. The applicant must provide a qualifying contract or binding job offer, professional-qualification evidence, a valid travel document, and sickness insurance where required.1

A university degree is often relevant, but the Commission also describes higher professional skills routes in certain occupations and where national law provides for them.1 This does not mean that professional experience replaces a degree in every country or occupation. Check the country page and the occupation-specific rules.

A job title such as “senior developer” or “project engineer” is not enough by itself. The contract, duties, qualification evidence, and salary must align.

Contract and salary evidence

The employment contract or binding offer should identify the employer, work location, job, salary, start date, and duration. The Commission’s general information states that the contract or offer should be for at least six months and that the salary must meet the applicable national threshold.1

Do not use an old salary table copied from a blog. Thresholds and national procedures can change. Find the current figure on the official authority for the destination country and retain the date of the information you used.

If the salary includes bonuses, equity, accommodation, or benefits, ask how the national authority counts each component. Do not assume that every form of compensation satisfies the threshold.

Qualification evidence and regulated professions

Prepare the diploma, transcript, professional certificate, or evidence of qualifying professional experience requested by the country. The Commission links applicants to information about recognition of qualifications and regulated professions.1

A regulated profession may require recognition or registration beyond the Blue Card application. For example, a professional title may be protected even if the role is advertised in English. The engineering jobs guide explains why technical fit and professional authorization should be checked separately.

If the occupation is not regulated, the employer and authority may still require evidence that your education or experience matches the role. Keep the evidence clear, translated where required, and consistent with the CV.

Where do you apply?

The European Commission states that the applicant or employer submits the EU Blue Card application to the competent national authorities in the Member State where the person wishes to work.1 The exact authority, online system, appointment, and responsibility vary.

Some countries allow the employer to apply or support the process; others require the worker to submit through a consulate or national authority. Use the country map and official national links on the European Commission portal, then check the embassy or immigration authority covering your residence.

Do not submit through a private website that claims to issue EU Blue Cards. The Commission expressly notes that only EU Member States can issue the card.1

How long does the EU Blue Card application take?

The European Commission’s general information states that applicants are entitled to a decision as soon as possible and no later than 90 days after submission of a complete application.1

Treat 90 days as a legal-information reference, not a travel guarantee. The practical timeline can include employer preparation, qualification recognition, translation, appointments, missing-document requests, visa processing, and national administrative steps.

Build a timeline with separate dates: job acceptance, document preparation, application submission, completeness confirmation, decision, visa appointment, travel, and residence-card collection.

What happens after approval?

Approval does not remove the need to follow the visa and entry instructions for your nationality. You may need a visa or a national long-stay document before entering the country.1

Read the approval notice carefully. It should identify the permitted work, employer, start date, and any conditions. After arrival, complete registration and residence formalities within the national deadline.

The legitimate jobs guide explains how to verify that the employer, contract, and official process are genuine before travel.

Changing jobs and moving to another EU country

The Blue Card can offer mobility rights, but it is not a free pass to work anywhere in Europe. The Commission states that the holder may need to notify national authorities about an employer change during the first 12 months, and that moving to another Member State generally requires a new EU Blue Card in the destination country.1

The Commission also describes a possible move to another Member State after 12 months of legal residence, subject to the destination country’s rules.1 Check the national procedure before accepting a cross-border transfer.

For more general job-search routes, read the Europe job seeker visa countries guide. It explains why a job-search route and a highly qualified employment route should not be mixed together.

Family and unemployment considerations

The European Commission describes more favourable family-reunification conditions for Blue Card holders when the relevant conditions are met, as well as different periods for finding a new job after involuntary unemployment depending on how long the holder has had the card.1

These rights have conditions and national procedures. Read the official notice for the Member State that issued the card. Do not assume that losing a job automatically preserves every right or that the same unemployment period applies in every situation.

A Blue Card application quality check

Before submission, conduct a consistency review.

Check Question
Identity Are names, dates of birth, and passport details identical everywhere?
Employment Do the title, duties, salary, location, and duration match?
Qualifications Does the evidence match the occupation and national recognition rule?
Language Are documents translated in the accepted format?
Authority Are you submitting to the correct national authority?
Timing Can you meet the visa, entry, and residence deadlines?
Mobility Do you understand the employer-change and country-change rules?

Do not submit until you can answer every question with a document or official instruction.

Frequently asked questions

Can I apply for an EU Blue Card without a job offer?

The general EU Blue Card conditions include a work contract or binding job offer for highly qualified employment for at least six months. It is not a general job-search visa.1

How long is the EU Blue Card decision period?

The European Commission states that a decision should be made as soon as possible and no later than 90 days after a complete application is submitted. Practical preparation and national steps can add time.1

Does the EU Blue Card work in every EU country?

The EU Blue Card framework described by the Commission applies in 25 of the 27 EU Member States; Denmark and Ireland do not participate in that framework.1

Can professional experience replace a degree?

In some occupations and under some national rules, higher professional skills or experience may be accepted. The answer is not universal; check the destination country and occupation.1

Can I change employer with an EU Blue Card?

You may be able to change jobs, but notification or approval can be required, especially during the first 12 months. Check the rules of the issuing Member State.1

Final takeaway

To apply for the EU Blue Card, start with a qualifying job in a specific Member State, verify the contract and salary, prepare qualification and insurance evidence, submit to the correct national authority, and follow the country’s visa and residence steps.

The Blue Card can support international mobility, but its value depends on matching the job, documents, employer, salary, and national rules. Use the European Commission portal as the starting point and the destination country’s authority as the final source.
