Types of Work Visas and Permits in Europe

7 min readUpdated 2026

One of the biggest sources of confusion when thinking about working in Europe is mixing up a "Schengen tourist visa" with a "work permit." These are not the same document, and a visitor visa never allows you to work.

Visas

1. Work Visa (With a Signed Contract)

The most common pathway: you receive a signed job offer from a company in your target country, and the company or you apply for a work permit with the relevant authority. Once approved, you apply for the visa at the embassy. This route usually requires the role to be on a list of in-demand professions, or the employer to prove no suitable local candidate was available.

2. EU Blue Card

Aimed at highly qualified candidates (typically a university degree or equivalent professional experience) with a job offer above a minimum salary threshold that varies by country. The Blue Card grants broader rights than a standard work permit, including easier movement between some EU countries after a period, and simplified family reunification.

3. Job Seeker Visa

Offered by a limited number of countries such as Germany, Austria, and Sweden, this lets you enter and stay for several months to search for work in person and attend interviews, provided you can prove your qualifications and cover your living costs during that period. If you land a job, the visa converts into a work permit.

4. Training or Vocational Permit

For internships or vocational training (such as Germany's Ausbildung) linked to your field of study or a recognized training program. This route is simpler than a full work visa, though limited in duration and often paid a modest stipend — and in some cases leads to a permanent contract afterward.

5. Seasonal Work Permit

Used mainly in sectors like agriculture, hospitality, and tourism during specific seasons (summer, harvest time). Short in duration and rarely leading to permanent residence, but a solid way to build European experience and a network.

Important: The specific requirements for each pathway (minimum salary, duration, required documents) vary by country and change over time. Treat this article as a starting point, and always check the official website of the country's embassy or immigration office before applying.

How to Choose the Right Pathway

  1. Assess your qualifications: a university degree, professional experience, or are you just starting out?
  2. Check the "in-demand professions" list for your target country, if one exists.
  3. Check whether the country offers a job-seeker visa, or requires a signed contract first.
  4. Calculate the cost of each pathway (fees, insurance, proof of funds) before committing.
